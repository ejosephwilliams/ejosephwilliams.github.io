Young adult women’s perspective of timing and appropriateness of preconception health messages

Main finding

Young women want preconception health education before pregnancy, tailored to their age and framed as whole women’s health, not just pregnancy prevention.

Background

Preconception care (PC) plays a critical role in improving maternal and infant health. Yet there is no clear consensus on the optimal timing of PC delivery to achieve the best outcomes. Because PC needs vary across individuals, message content and timing must be tailored accordingly.

This study explored young women’s perspectives on the timing and appropriateness of PC messaging.

Methods

  • Guided by the socioecological model.
  • Six individual semistructured interviews and eight focus groups, conducted between November 2025 and February 2026.
  • Data collected by videoconference. Questions covered preconception health knowledge, exposure to services, and the timing of preconception health education. Interview questions were theoretically developed.
  • Data analyzed with inductive thematic analysis.

Results: four themes

  1. Age-based PC messaging and education

    “Starting young, it’s just kind of general information… around our ages… providers should ask the specific questions.”

  2. Providers and family as a source of PC education

    “…so she is up to date on what I am doing and how she might need to adjust my care or my medication based on… what I’m doing in my daily life.”

  3. Motivators influencing PC use

    “I’ve been getting like advertisements for understanding your forms of preconception on Snapchat and would say [is] also another motivator [for preconception health].”

  4. Perceived barriers to PC

    “…they’re very likely to… push off your symptoms sometimes… But a lot of times there’s underlying conditions in that.”

Participant quotes. An ellipsis (…) marks omitted words, and square brackets mark words added for clarity.

Conclusions

Women in this study favored age-appropriate PC education delivered before pregnancy or childbirth. Their narratives challenge current practices that emphasize pregnancy-prevention messaging, particularly among college-aged women, and instead highlight the need to frame PC as a holistic component of women’s health.

Implications

Integrating sexual, physical, and mental health within PC messaging may better support maternal and infant health, especially for women who are not actively planning pregnancy.
