Does social support buffer the effects of precarious employment? Evidence among working mothers

← All posters

This page is a screen-reader-friendly text version of the poster.

Main finding

Working mothers who did not report financial, emotional or childcare-arrangement support had up to 2.9 times the odds of depression and anxiety. There was no evidence that support buffered the association between employment precarity and mental health.

Background

Social support systems, including community and family networks, are crucial for maternal mental health. This study examines whether social support buffers the association between employment precarity and mental health among working mothers with children under age 5.

Methods

  • Cross-sectional online survey of 530 working mothers with children under age 5 (Qualtrics, October to November 2025).
  • Outcomes: depression (PHQ-9) and anxiety (GAD-7), each defined as a score of 10 or above.
  • Social support was measured as help received from family, a spouse or partner, or others (“choose all that apply”) in three domains: financial, emotional and arranging childcare. Mothers who did not check a type of help were compared with those who did. An unchecked box cannot distinguish having no help from not needing it.
  • Employment precarity measured with the Employment Precariousness Scale (EPRES) and grouped as moderate/high versus none/low.
  • Multivariable logistic regression adjusted for race, age, education, household income, family size, marital status, geographic region and number of children under five. Models include 529 of 530 mothers because one respondent had no income value.

Did support buffer the effect of precarious employment?

No. Interaction terms between each type of support and employment precarity were not statistically significant (all p ≥ 0.13). This suggests that the association of support with mental health did not vary by precarity. The confidence intervals for the interaction terms were wide, so a buffering effect cannot be ruled out.

Race

Compared with non-Hispanic White mothers, non-Hispanic Black mothers had higher odds of depression (aOR 1.76, 1.62 and 1.71 in the financial, emotional and childcare models) and anxiety (aOR 1.80, 1.64 and 1.76), all p < 0.05. These estimates come from the models that include the support by precarity interaction.

The gap between mothers who did and did not report help looked similar at both precarity levels; the interaction tests were not significant (all p ≥ 0.13).

Limitations

The survey was cross-sectional, so associations cannot show cause and effect. The sample included only Black and White women. Support was a check-all item, so an unchecked box may mean a mother did not need that help rather than that she lacked it. Confidence intervals for the interaction tests were wide, so a buffering effect cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

Working mothers who reported financial, emotional or childcare support had lower odds of depression and anxiety, and this association did not differ by employment precarity. Although we found no evidence that support buffered the effects of precarity, policies that strengthen access to these supports could benefit working mothers, particularly non-Hispanic Black mothers.

PHQ-9 = Patient Health Questionnaire-9. GAD-7 = Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7. aOR = adjusted odds ratio. Poster presented at the APHA Annual Meeting, San Antonio, TX, November 2026.
